# Product lens
Score: 4/10 | Vote: FUND IF

## What is strong
- **The one thing is clear.** "Make the existing path speak. No app. No battery. No renovation." It fits in a sentence, and the sentence says what the product leaves out. Few early ideas get this far.
- **It starts from the experience.** The insight begins with a hand on a rail: the rail is already in the hand and it is blank. That is working back from the moment of use, not forward from a technology.
- **The tactile grammar is real product thinking.** Ribs that tell the hand "information, safety or end of stairs" before it reads anything are the kind of detail that shows taste. If it works, it is the part a copy would get wrong.
- **One module for every level of sight.** Braille (Arabic built in) for people who are blind, high contrast and raised numbers for people with low vision, and a glow for everyone in a blackout. One object, no extra system for the building to run.

## What would kill it (the top 3, most dangerous first)
1. **Nobody who will read it has touched it.** No user testing with people who are blind or have low vision has happened. The whole promise (the hand finds it without searching, the ribs can be told apart, the braille reads cleanly on a curved rail of 30–50 mm, Arabic braille is right) is untested. If the read fails, there is no product, however good the sales story.
2. **The object is not specified, and it sits on a safety rail.** Material, braille dimensions, glow performance, durability, print time and failure rate are all [TO CONFIRM], and no durability test is logged. A handrail is what someone grabs when they slip. A clip that loosens, turns, cracks or catches a hand turns an accessibility product into a hazard and a liability. Certification has not started and the authority is unknown, yet it sits at Month 6 on the roadmap.
3. **Too many mounts, too many seams.** Seven mounting contexts (stairs, door levers, hospital rails in three profiles, metro, bus, airport, malls) across eight sectors, each measured on site and printed to fit, then installed, mapped and kept up under a care plan. Every site visit, custom print and install crew is a handoff where quality can slip. Nothing in the brief says who checks a clip is still there and still right a year later, or what happens when a room number changes.

## The questions I would ask the founder
- Describe the first five minutes for a person who is blind arriving at an unfamiliar stairwell with DALIL. What does the hand meet, in what order, and how do they know the sign is complete?
- If you could ship only one mount for the first year, which is it? What are you cutting?
- What is the braille spec (dot height, spacing, standard followed), and has anyone who reads Arabic braille checked a printed sample?
- What does a clip do when someone puts their full weight on the rail, or when it is cleaned with hospital disinfectant every day?
- Who in the building notices a missing or wrong clip, and how fast is it replaced?
- Material cost and print time per clip, and the cost of measuring each site: open, answered by **/founder-cfo**. Whether the 58% margin holds once custom fitting is costed is the same question.
- What wall tactile signs really cost and how long they take to install: open, answered by **/founder-competitors**.
- Whether a facilities manager pays per floor before certification: open, a first read from **/founder-consumer**. Simulated buyers cannot stand in for real people who are blind or have low vision testing the product.

## If FUND IF: the conditions
1. **One mount, one site.** Pick the stair handrail on the founders' campus and cut everything else until it works.
2. **Real users first.** Hold at least the first planned round of testing with people who are blind or have low vision, on printed clips, before any sale. Record find time, read accuracy for each rib type and comfort of grip, and publish what failed.
3. **A written v1 spec with logged tests**: braille dimensions, grip-load and pull-off, cleaning-chemical and wear tests, glow duration. Pass/fail thresholds set before testing.
4. **Name the certification route** (which approvals, which authority) and get a UAE accredited lab's view on whether a clip on a handrail needs one, before the Month 6 date is promised.
5. **Map the experience end to end** (site visit, measure, print, install, map, care check) with one owner for each handoff, and a replacement process for a missing clip.
