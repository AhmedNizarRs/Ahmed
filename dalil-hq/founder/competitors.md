# DALIL · The competition

Run: 2026-11-08, `/founder-competitors`. Public sources only; every row in `competitors.csv` has its link. Where a source could not be reached or a fact was not found, it says so. Quotes are verbatim research, never testimonials.

## The short version

1. **Braille on handrails already exists.** At least six makers sell it: in Germany, Serbia, China, Singapore and India. A German product is sold in the Gulf through a Saudi distributor and comes in **photoluminescent plastic**. So "braille + handrail + glow" is not new on its own.
2. **It is already in the UAE.** Dubai Municipality holds a Guinness World Record for the world's longest braille handrail (319.11 m, Dubai Frame, 2023). Dubai Municipality also introduced NaviLens, a phone-camera wayfinding system, in October 2026.
3. **Basic wall signs cost about what a DALIL clip costs.** A UAE directory guide gives AED 80–150 for a basic indoor braille sign and AED 180–350+ for metal or ADA-grade. DALIL's AED 130 sits inside the basic range, not below it. `DALIL-facts.md` compares only with the AED 180–400 end.
4. **The strongest objection is to the category, not to a rival.** A UK access consultant reports that the RNIB (the UK's national sight-loss charity) does not recommend tactile data on handrails. Braille Literacy Canada says braille should only go on a flat part of the rail, not on the slope of the stair.
5. **The gap is narrower than the pitch, and it is real.** We found no UAE maker of handrail braille, no handrail product that says it has Arabic braille, and no fit that needs no drilling or screws. Nobody has published user-testing evidence that handrail braille works on stairs. DALIL can own all four.

## 4. What people say about the alternatives

There are almost no public customer reviews of signage suppliers: buyers are facilities teams, and B2B sign makers do not collect reviews. What does exist is (a) app-store reviews of the apps and (b) published views from user organisations and access professionals on handrail braille. Both are below, ranked by how much they matter to DALIL.

### a. "Handrail braille may not help, and may slow people on stairs" — expert and user-group views, not customer reviews

The most important finding in this research.

- The RNIB "takes the view that tactile data is not useful and does not recommend its provision", reported by UK access consultant Ian Streets, who adds: "A limited sample of visually impaired people raised doubts as to the usefulness of providing such data and said they wouldn't use it or find it useful." ([PBC Today, 2023](https://www.pbctoday.co.uk/news/building-control-news/adding-tactile-data-handrails-might-not-come-in-handy/129951/))
- Safety on the stair: "if people were to slow down or stop, it could create a safety or trip hazard for others following behind." (same source)
- Few people read braille: the article cites the RNIB's estimate that "about 7% of visually impaired people read braille" in the UK and the US National Federation of the Blind's "less than 10%". (same source, secondhand)
- Where to put it: "Only place braille on handrails if the floor surface is flat to allow for safe standing while reading. This means handrails on stairs should have extensions where braille and tactile print can be placed." Braille Literacy Canada recommends extensions of at least 300 mm at both ends. ([Braille Literacy Canada, Accessible Signage Guidelines 2024](https://www.brailleliteracycanada.ca/storage/standards/BLC%20Accessible%20Signage%20Guidelines%202024.pdf))
- Consistency: "not providing it in some locations would leave data gaps in the information system that people would be looking for if they rely on it." (PBC Today)

Against that, the same article reports that the ISO standard says tactile information with braille "shall be unobtrusively and permanently fitted or fixed at the beginning and at the end of every handrail on stairs, indicating at least the floor number". So there is a standard behind the idea and an expert debate about whether it helps. That debate is DALIL's opening. Theme strength: two independent organisations; no UAE voice yet.

### b. Phone apps depend on the phone and the setup — thin (3 reviews)

- "I didn't foresee the 'no information available' message when scanning the sticker on the tram." ([NaviLens, Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.neosistec.NaviLens&hl=en_US))
- "When the talkback is on in my phone the application's voice is turned off." (same)
- "you have to print your own labels ... I would need both a printer and sighted help to create my own tags, negating independent living." ([NaviLens, App Store UK](https://apps.apple.com/gb/app/navilens/id1273704914?see-all=reviews&platform=iphone)). This one is about labelling at home, not building wayfinding.

NaviLens rates 3.9 on Google Play (292 reviews) and 3.9 on the App Store UK (15 ratings). This supports DALIL's "no app, no battery" line, but only weakly: three reviews is thin.

### c. Arabic on tactile signs is awkward to make — one supplier's view, thin

A UAE company selling braille sign printers writes: "Bilingual Arabic and English layouts and constant one-off replacements are what break the drilled-and-glued method", and "Arabic Braille has its own conventions." ([PRO TECHnology](https://www.protechnology.ae/braille-tactile-signage-printing-uae)). It is a seller's claim, but it points to Arabic braille as a real pain point.

### d. What the code asks for in Dubai

The Dubai Universal Design Code, in its handrails section, says braille and tactile signage "shall be provided on the door jamb at a height of 1500 mm". On signs: "If signage is supplemented with Braille, it should be located at the bottom of the sign and presented in Grade One Braille that meets the standards in English." ([Dubai Universal Design Code, hosted by DHA](https://www.dha.gov.ae/uploads/112021/2b372137-cade-4be0-88a2-4828fac05deb.pdf)). Two consequences for DALIL:

- In Dubai, the code puts the sign on the **door jamb**, not the rail. A DALIL clip may sit **next to** a required wall or door sign, not replace it. This answers one of the board's questions, for Dubai: add-on, not replacement, unless a code professional says otherwise.
- The code asks for **English** Grade 1 braille. "Arabic braille built in" is a strength for users, but a building that needs to comply will want English too.

Not found: a Sharjah-specific accessibility code, and what the UAE Fire and Life Safety Code says about photoluminescent marking (the code is a 1,348-page PDF that could not be read in this round). Both are `[TO CONFIRM]` with a professional.

## 5. The gap

**There is a gap, but it is not "nobody puts braille on handrails".** That claim would be false: it exists in at least six countries, in the Gulf through a Saudi distributor, and in Dubai at the Dubai Frame.

What nobody in this research does:

1. **Local and measured to fit.** No UAE maker of handrail braille was found. The overseas makers quote on request and ship metal or plastic plates; local sign shops make wall signs. DALIL measures on site in Sharjah and prints to fit. (Evidence: no UAE supplier found; overseas makers are in Germany, Serbia, China, Singapore and India.)
2. **No drilling.** The fitting methods we could read need screws (XiongChang) or a drilled hole (Tactile Systems Australia). A clip that snaps on with no tools matters in a building that cannot drill its rails, and for a pilot that can be removed. (Evidence: two of the products' own pages; the others did not state a method.)
3. **Arabic braille on the rail.** None of the handrail products says it offers Arabic braille. A UAE printer seller calls bilingual Arabic plates awkward to make. (Evidence: product pages; PRO TECHnology.)
4. **Proof that it works.** The category's critics (RNIB, Braille Literacy Canada) doubt that handrail braille helps on stairs. Nobody we found has published tests with people who are blind or have low vision showing that it does. DALIL's planned user testing could become the evidence the whole category lacks, and the tactile grammar (ribs that say the message type before the braille) is a direct answer to the "too slow to read on a stair" objection.

What is **not** a gap:
- The glow. Ellis already makes photoluminescent handrail plates.
- Price, against basic wall signs. AED 130 is inside the AED 80–150 basic range. The cheaper-than-wall-signs story only holds against metal or ADA-grade signs, and against the hospital comparison already in `DALIL-facts.md`.
- "Found by the hand." CDA already says: "Floor number and direction, where the hand already is."

## 6. The threat: who could copy DALIL fastest

1. **UAE sign makers with UV braille printers.** PRO TECHnology sells machines that print raised text and braille, including bilingual Arabic and English plates, with no tooling. Any of the sign shops already selling braille signs to the same facilities teams could add a rail plate in weeks. The defence is the clip's fit and the tactile grammar, protected by a design filing (`[TO CONFIRM]`), and being the name tied to the evidence.
2. **Al Bassam / Ellis.** A Gulf distributor already sells German handrail plates, glow included, in Arabic-speaking markets. Extending into the UAE is a small step.
3. **Whoever built the Dubai Frame handrail.** They have a government reference in the UAE already. Finding out who it was is worth doing: a competitor, a partner, or a sign that Dubai Municipality will write handrail braille into its own projects.
4. **NaviLens with Dubai Municipality.** Not a copy, but it competes for the same accessibility budget, with a government endorsement since October 2026.
